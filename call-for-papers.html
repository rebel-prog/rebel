Call for papers

The aim of the workshop is to bring together different strands of criticism of the way in which generative linguistics — understood as an institutionalised practice — delineates its target phenomenon: natural language grammar.

Background

What counts as part of natural language grammar, and what does not, has never been entirely self-evident. The boundaries of the field have been contested, negotiated, and reshaped over time, influenced by disciplinary conventions, institutional structures, social hierarchies, and power relations. These inclusions and exclusions also determine what we accept as evidence — which observations are treated as direct evidence about grammar, and which are instead viewed as noise, performance effects, confounds, or individual variation.

Sign languages, for example, were excluded from being “languages proper” until far into the 20th century, and arguably still are in many ways (Woll 2013). Their inclusion has expanded the empirical domain of linguistics and forced a partial reconceptualisation of assumptions underlying the notion of natural language — including the historical privileging of speech and the vocal modality as the prototypical form of language (Armstrong, Stokoe & Wilcox 1995).

At the same time, this overdue acknowledgement rests on a particular conception of grammar as based on universal rules, a particular conception of compositionality and fluency, and a separation of form, meaning, and “non-linguistic” factors. Only because sign languages conform to these other criteria did it become obvious that their earlier exclusion had rested on bias and the exercise of societal power.

Most of us routinely assume that participants have “genuinely linguistic intuitions” which can be neatly separated — at least in theory — from various “outside” factors, and then used to support our abstract theories. Such outside factors and reasons for exclusion can include:

  • communicative intentions falling outside the received scope of pragmatics (e.g. in language arts and play; McCready 2025);
  • conscious decisions to speak or teach language in a new way (e.g. neopronouns; Pike-Acosta et al. 2025; Kolek 2022; or singular they; Arnold 2026);
  • non-native acquisition;
  • non-belonging to the human species;
  • neurodivergence;
  • aphasia and other pathologised states of the brain and mind, whose data are often treated as informative mainly insofar as they illuminate “healthy” language (Garraffa & Fyndanis 2020).

The marginalised status of these fields can have fundamentally different characteristics and justifications — differences which may themselves be a topic of the workshop.

Questions we want to explore

We particularly welcome contributions addressing questions such as:

  • How have particular historical, social, or theoretical exclusions shaped what counts as a legitimate object of linguistic inquiry?
  • What should count as natural language grammar? For each phenomenon currently treated as separate from mainstream linguistics: should it be incorporated into the core object of study — and what would “yes”, “no”, or “partially” imply for the discipline?
  • What counts as linguistic evidence? Which kinds of data should motivate us to change the boundaries of the field, and how should we treat data that do not conform to established notions of intuition, competence, fluency, or acceptability?
  • How radical should a rethinking of linguistic research be — inclusion versus deconstruction?
  • How should we position linguistic research in light of contemporary anti-ableist discourse, especially when researching neurodivergence, aphasia, and related areas?
  • How can we move away from the historical over-focus on “literal” meaning while accounting for the varying significance of literalism across neurotypes?
  • How should social and contextual factors — context, speaker beliefs, common ground, stereotypicality — enter linguistic theory in ways that take social, decolonial, and related disciplines seriously?
  • What do we do when we ask participants “can you say that?” or “does that sound natural?” Which participants can readily answer such questions, and which cannot or might not want to?
  • What would a genuinely different linguistic methodology look like, if categories such as “speaker”, “intuition”, “acceptability”, “competence”, or “performance” are themselves shaped by disciplinary assumptions?
  • How can we take the diversity of language competence and usage seriously without falling into exoticism or western-biased universalism (cf. the Everett–Chomsky debate on Pirahã; Nevins et al. 2009; Sauerland 2018)?

Scope: what fits, and what does not

Because this is a broad scope of topics and ways of thinking about exclusion, it matters to us to be clear about what we do not consider a good fit.

A good fit

  • Work that engages explicitly with linguistic theory.
  • Work that critically examines the assumptions, categories, boundaries, or evidential practices underlying linguistic research.
  • Any language variety, modality, population, phenomenon, or type of data, drawing on any area of linguistics.
  • Philosophical and foundational critique grounded in an understanding of linguistics as a discipline.

Not a good fit

  • Research within a marginalised area that does not discuss its impact on, or relationship to, mainstream linguistics (e.g. a study on autistic implicature processing that does not challenge neuronormative theories of implicature).
  • General language-philosophical talks that do not position themselves, in an informed way, towards concrete contemporary linguistic practice.

Abstract submission

We welcome submissions that engage with the questions and themes above. Contributions may address any language variety, modality, population, linguistic phenomenon, or type of data, and may draw on any area of linguistics. Submissions should engage explicitly with linguistic theory and critically examine the assumptions, categories, boundaries, or evidential practices underlying linguistic research.

We particularly encourage submissions from early-career researchers, including advanced BA/MA students and PhD candidates.

All submitted abstracts undergo double-blind peer review by at least two reviewers. In addition, we may consult self-advocate groups and individuals — for example, neurodivergent scholars or native signers — and consider their view on an anonymised abstract in our final decision. The submission portal will give authors the non-mandatory option to indicate personal affectedness by their research topic; reviewers will not have access to this information.

How to submit

Submit an anonymous abstract of up to 1000 words (excluding examples and references) in PDF format via EasyAbs. Participants may submit up to two abstracts, but at most one may be single-authored.
